**The question it answers:** conceptual change, "How did people come to think this way, and where did the old idea fall
short?" ([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)). Afterwards the viewer can say why the old
idea was reasonable, where it broke down, and what the new idea gains and costs.

**How it meets the must-haves:** the old idea is first rebuilt as reasonable, using what people at the time could see;
where it breaks down is a concrete counterexample or pressure; the cost of the new idea is stated too.

**Use it for:** the history of mathematics, of science, of economic thought, the evolution of legal theory, and any
content about "how an idea was changed". The model came from a series on the history of mathematical modernism, so most
examples are from the history of mathematics; for another subject, keep the structure and change the examples.

**Not this one:** a question still unsettled today, or a debate among historians, is perspectives; an overview of many
events over a long period is narrative. Theo has no playbook for either ([presenter.md](../presenter.md)); choose
another presenter.

The hard part of the history of ideas is **knowing what to put on screen**. This document takes on that
question. It has only one principle:

> **Every historical element (a person, a year, a text, an argument, a debate) must be an object with a job to do on
> screen, not decoration.**

---

**Versus other presenters' conceptual change playbooks:** a conceptual change question goes to Theo by default.
Three cases go to someone else ([presenters/README.md](../../README.md)):

- The old intuition is still in the viewer's head, and was later overturned by an institution or a ruling (for example
  a legal intuition) → Sophie.
- The change of idea falls on two dated decisions → Clio
  ([presenters/clio/question-types/conceptual-change.md](../../clio/question-types/conceptual-change.md)).
- The old idea is an old method that can be built → decide by the presenter probe, Theo or Mason.

## Skeleton

[The core translation](#the-core-translation-from-mechanism-to-the-pressure-that-forced-the-step) below is the chain
every shift walks through; this table lays it out as one episode. Basis: the actual sections of two well-received
episodes made with this playbook (conceptual changes in biology, 8 to 10 minutes each), measured as shares of narration
length.

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | A question, and what two answers look like | The first 5 seconds are something concrete and strange (hundreds of things that used to be bacteria live in every cell of your body; a spore lay in the permafrost for thousands of years and woke up); the main question in one sentence, made into a question card that stays in the corner for the whole episode; the old answer (textbook, intuition) and the new one, one concrete picture each, so the viewer sees them first |
| 8–25% | The parts the new idea needs come on first (when needed) | Build what the new account will use (a new kind of organism, a quantity), with the subject itself done once on screen (compare a gene, compute an entropy); plant a clue to pay off later. Leave this out when not needed, and give the time to the old idea |
| 25–40% | The old idea made reasonable | Act it out from the viewer's daily life or from what people then could see (a room left alone gets messy; a cell that swallows things swallows a bacterium); say its strongest reason; name it only once understood; lay it out as a picture that will be changed later (an order of three cells, an equation) |
| 40–50% | The old idea's own prediction | What it predicts we should see; first give its evidence fairly (the group taken back then for survivors), then test it by its own prediction; when the viewer would really be curious, ask once ("If you grind up the spore, how much do you think the disorder increases?") |
| 50–65% | It breaks: on a concrete object | The hardest step of the video: a concrete counterexample or pressure, acted out on something the viewer has seen (vinaigrette separating by itself); one beat per link; say what is not yet settled |
| 65–82% | The new idea, rebuilt on the same object | The new account and one piece of its evidence (paying off the clue planted earlier), changing the order or the equation in the same picture; when needed, give it weight with real numbers (the whole body turns over about a body weight of ATP a day) |
| 82–92% | Transfer, and where the old idea was right | Hand the viewer the question this episode used to judge, on another example, ideally getting the opposite answer, or exactly where the old account holds (chloroplasts, snowflakes); say which part of the old idea is still right |
| 92–100% | Close: what it gains, what it costs | What the new idea gains and what is still unknown (the author's speculation marked as a hypothesis); answer the corner question with the same sentence; hand off to the next episode |

In both episodes, what viewers commented on most were sentences added after the script audience review, and reasoning
links not taken apart in the "it breaks" step (four links packed into one step). So the plan's notes for this step
write how many links it holds (the links in [teaching/plan.md](../../../teaching/plan.md#what-each-episode-records)), and
the script gives each link one beat.

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

- The old idea made reasonable, it breaking on an object, the new idea rebuilt in the same picture: one beat each;
  transfer in one sentence.
- Leave out the parts passage; the "it breaks" step can never be cut.

## The core translation: from "mechanism" to "the pressure that forced the step"

3b1b's physics and AI videos explain a "mechanism". A history of ideas video must explain **why a conceptual shift had
to happen**. Every shift goes through this chain:

```
A research question (concrete, drawable)
  → the old concept breaks down here (draw the object on which it fails)
    → the new definition (rebuilt on the same object)
      → the price paid (opponents, paradoxes, anxiety, who was offended)
        → what we inherit today (which page of your textbook)
```

- **The form of the punchline:** "So **this** is why your analysis course defines ℝ before it can prove anything about
  limits."
- **The standard of explanation** (instead of "it feels discovered"): "If you were a mathematician (scientist, jurist…)
  of the time, facing the same pressure, would you have made the same choice? And why would a reasonable contemporary
  resist?"
- **The foil** is usually the Whig-history standard story: "the field kept progressing and people finally got it right"
  (in the history of mathematics: "mathematics kept getting more rigorous and people finally got it right"). State it
  fairly, then prosecute it: it makes every step fall out of the sky and makes the opponents look like fools.

### The "What is ___?" mini-act template

For chapters where a series of concepts is redefined one after another (for example geometry, the straight line, the
real numbers, the integers, number, existence, mathematics):

1. **The naive answer** (the one the viewer would give; best to let them guess first).
2. **The object that breaks it** (a concrete construction on which the naive answer fails).
3. **The restated answer** (the new definition, built on the same object).
4. **The cost** (who objected, why, and their strongest argument).
5. **If the series has a running list, add this question to it** (accumulating episode by episode like the GPT-3
   parameter table, as the thread through the series; scene_kit's `get_running_list`).

## People

- **3 to 5 people with a real role per episode;** everyone else appears only as a "name chip" on the timeline.
- Each person gets **one vivid, verifiable detail that bears on the argument** (for example Klein a full professor at
  23; Poincaré placed only second because he could not draw; Lie publicly humiliating Klein in a preface; Cantor's
  first breakdown in 1884). The detail serves the argument; it is not gossip.
- People **enter when needed and leave when done.** No biographical timeline.
- **People stand for positions:** present Kronecker vs Dedekind, du Bois-Reymond's idealist vs empiricist, Frege vs
  Thomae as **models carrying assumptions**, each in its strongest version (steelman).
- **Let a real historical sceptic be the "client"** (the pattern of the Wigner friend in the π video): for example
  Kronecker or Frege, who comes back at the end of every act to "check" whether the new definition passes.

## Years and geography

- **A persistent timeline object:** a number line with year ticks; people and texts are points or cards on it. Between
  sections the camera pans along the timeline; it returns as a callback ("you are here: 1872"). **Years become
  positions, not text.**
- **A map** (when needed): European cities are nodes (Berlin, Göttingen, Paris, Turin, Halle, Jena, Leipzig, Erlangen),
  coloured by school; letters are envelopes flying between cities; journals (Crelle, Acta Mathematica, Mathematische
  Annalen) are physical objects into which papers are "submitted" or from which they are "returned". Suits academic
  politics (Kronecker blocking Cantor's paper, Mittag-Leffler's journal).
- Keep the number of years in check: at most 5 years per episode that really need remembering; the rest are only
  positions on the timeline.

## Drawing arguments and conceptual shifts

| What to draw | How |
|---|---|
| A proof | A chain of boxes and arrows; future steps appear dimmed first; every step is actually carried out on the spine object |
| The diagonal argument | A grid of digits, the diagonal turning yellow, rewritten digit by digit |
| A bijection (interval ↔ square) | An animation of the decimal digits interleaving like a riffle shuffle |
| "Interchangeability of elements" (Enriques; Hilbert's tables, chairs and beer mugs; Wiener's sociological reading) | **The same picture, the same axioms, the labels swapped, and the theorems still hold:** labels replaced one by one while the theorem box keeps its green tick |
| A change of definition | `TransformMatchingTex` from the old definition to the new, so the viewer sees what is kept and what is replaced; or strike through and replace: "Geometry is about ~~space~~ → groups" |
| The expansion of a concept (integer, prime, existence) | Concentric circles or a set diagram growing from small to large, the old things becoming special cases of the new; the word being expanded ("integer") physically stretched wider on screen |
| Two opposing views | Layout T5 ([teaching/visual.md](../../../teaching/visual.md#layout-templates-169)), split screen: "intuitive" on the left, "formal" on the right; when the formal side is declared to take priority, the left dims but does not disappear |
| The hierarchy of a research programme (Klein's hierarchy of geometries) | The inclusions of groups drawn as nested boxes or a tree, each level labelled with that geometry's invariants |
| A paradox | The self-referential construction actually drawn (Russell's set: a set trying to put itself in or take itself out, the arrow reversing endlessly), ending in a red ✗ |
| The meaning of "existence" thinning out | An object's "solidity" visibly fading: solid object → wireframe → only the outline of a set of axioms (echoing Bôcher's "empty plate") |
| Academic acceptance | A network on the timeline: who cited whom, who blocked whose paper |

**Contrast templates** (taken from 3b1b): a restated-question card ("What is space?" dims → "Which axioms determine a
geometry?"), "then / now", "the convenient lie / the truth", strike-through and replace.

## Devices across the series

A history of ideas series particularly suits "devices that accumulate across episodes" as its thread, for example:

1. **A running list:** accumulating question by question on one side of the screen ("What is ___?" → the new answer).
   scene_kit: `get_running_list`.
2. **A ledger** (modelled on the GPT-3 parameter table): rows are fields, columns are "the old view / the new view / the
   key text", filled in chapter by chapter; the current row white, the others dimmed. scene_kit: `get_ledger`.
3. **A series map with "you are here";** each episode opens with a 2×2 or three-part roadmap, paid off at the end.
   scene_kit: `get_series_map`.
4. **Fixed school colours:** one colour per school or position, fixed in the series bible, used only on the
   commentary layer (portrait card borders, the ledger, key words in quotations).

## The questioner

3b1b's π creatures are its brand characters (and the artwork is not public), so **never reuse them**. The options
(decided in the series bible):

- **A. An original character:** a character drawn directly in code that echoes the series' theme (for example a small
  ghost), asking on the commentary layer for the viewer (or for the people of the time): "Isn't this circular?", "So
  what is a number, really?". Define the character in the series' shared code (`production/shared/<name>.py`); bring it
  in and out with scene_kit's `play_questioner_entrance`, `play_questioner_question` and `play_questioner_exit`.
- **B. No character:** use a question card plus narration that says "you might be thinking…".

The rules do not change: once every 2 to 4 minutes, 5 to 15 seconds each time, **it only asks and never answers**, on the
dark grey commentary layer, with the scene shrunk into an inset.

## Do not lose the subject itself

- **Every episode carries at least one passage of real subject content done step by step on screen.** The history of
  ideas is about ideas, but the ideas are about some subject, so let the viewer see that subject itself at work. In the
  history of mathematics it is a derivation (see "the core that cannot be dropped" in
  [subjects/mathematics.md](../../../subjects/mathematics.md#6-the-core-that-cannot-be-dropped)), in the history of science an experiment or calculation,
  in legal history one complete application of a rule to facts, in philosophy an argument built and tested step by
  step.
- **Reframe the close onto something the viewer knows now:** which page of the textbook, which habit at work, is really
  what this history left behind.

## Common pitfalls

- Whig history as the argument (it should be the foil).
- Too many names and years.
- Philosophy with no object (first let the viewer see a construction whose existence is in question).
- Losing the mathematics (see [do not lose the subject itself](#do-not-lose-the-subject-itself)).
- Building slides out of portraits and quotations.
- Judging the past by the present: playing the opponents as fools.

## People and the historian's techniques

From studying how 3Blue1Brown handles people and history:

- **A person enters with one vivid, verifiable detail:** Hamilton's son asking every day "Well, Papa, can you multiply
  triplets?"; Herschel's blueprints and moons; the Bernoulli brothers' feud.
- **Discounted legends:** "The story goes…", "This may be a legend, but it makes the point."
- **Anachronism warnings:** "Nobody then would have called this a function"; corrected attribution: Raphson simplified
  Newton's method.
- **"Imagine you lived then, and knew only this."**
- **Explain why the textbook looks the way it does, and admit the cost** (for example: "it reads like a riddle; it
  confused me too").

## Grant on history

- **History:** "discovery fiction" may depart from the real history, but must say so honestly (for example saying
  plainly that the story is a simplification, not the path history took). The first version of an idea is rarely the
  clearest, so historical order is not teaching order. See through mythologized legends, and verify every anecdote.

## Self-check

- [ ] 3 to 5 people with a real role per episode, everyone else only a name chip on the timeline?
- [ ] At most 5 years that really need remembering?
- [ ] Every quotation at most 2 lines of on-screen text, treated as "text as object"?
- [ ] Opponents presented in their strongest version?
- [ ] Legends discounted, anachronism warned about, the author's argument separated from the consensus, sources thanked
