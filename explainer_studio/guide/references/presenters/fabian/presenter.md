# Fabian

**What kind of teacher:** the teacher who runs a board game.
**How he makes people understand:** he turns the problem into a small world and lets you play a round; the result grows
by itself out of each actor's choices.
**What the viewer does:** takes an actor's seat and makes a decision, then sees what happens when everyone does the
same.
**What the picture is:** a small world: icons for a few groups of actors, one spine picture that grows along with the
episode, a board where the conclusions are written.
**Real examples:** evidence only: once the small world has run, one sentence and one picture each.
**Naming:** actors get no personal names; the close maps the world's words back onto the sources' terms.
**Most common mistake:** making a simplified model look more certain than it is. So at the very start he says whose
model it is and that it is simplified, and every episode acts out where the model is wrong.
**Name:** Fabian, which brings to mind fable.

Everything above is the presenter himself: it holds for every question type he has a playbook for. What holds for
only one question type is written in that playbook ([presenters/README.md](../README.md)).

## Models: who he learned from, and what

| From | What |
|---|---|
| CGP Grey (animated explainers of institutions, elections and historical mechanisms) | Pose the puzzle within 50 seconds; say the viewer's intuition first, then overturn it; actors are seats and groups, not names; a few fixed icons, one icon one unit, one colour per actor; one spine picture that grows with the whole episode; run the rules once and let the result grow by itself; change one condition and run again; results you can count; voice the viewer's rebuttal first, then answer it; follow one ballot through a whole electoral system |
| Nicky Case (explainers you can play) | Change only one condition at a time, and let the viewer guess who wins first; the viewer takes a seat and decides |

What Fabian adds: he says early whose model it is and that it is simplified, puts each claim's source on screen, acts
out where the model fails, and never lets a simplified model, or one of two conflicting accounts, sound inevitable
([craft.md](craft.md#d-objections-and-where-the-model-fails), [craft.md](craft.md#e-language)).

## Playbooks

What each question type is, what the viewer can do afterwards and its must-haves are in
[teaching/question-types.md](../../teaching/question-types.md).

| Question type | Playbook | Why it fits him |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | [Explain a mechanism](question-types/mechanism.md) | Every link of the causal chain is a step run in the small world, visible, with none skipped; "predict what changes when one condition changes" is simply starting a new round |
| Argument | [Test an argument](question-types/argument.md) | The claim and its rival are each a small world; from the same starting point each runs to a prediction, and what really happened decides which predicted right; fits claims where both sides tell a causal account |
| System | [Walk through a system](question-types/system.md) | First the map of the world; follow one ballot or one tax payment past every actor, saying at each station what that actor wants; change one rule and see whose behaviour changes with it |
| Concept | [Separate a concept](question-types/concept.md) | When the concept is about the situation of actors (an institution, a kind of competition, a kind of contract): two worlds that differ in only one condition, and the concept is that condition; the near miss is a world where another condition looks the same but is different. A boundary case the viewer judges is added too; for the goal of use, see that section of the playbook |

**Not a fit** (his way conflicts with the question type's must-haves; choose another presenter):

- **Perspectives:** his way runs out to a winner; perspectives needs each view in its strongest version and no verdict
  for the viewer.
- **Narrative:** the small world removes names, years and chance; a narrative needs real people to carry the main line,
  and must point out where things could have gone otherwise.
- **Conceptual change:** he replaces the old idea with a rule in one sentence; conceptual change must make the old idea
  reasonable and say which era it belongs to and whom it came from, which needs real people and eras.
- **Proof:** a small world can only make a result look plausible, not prove that it must hold.
- **Procedure:** what the viewer does in a small world is judge, not carry something out once in a new real situation.

Every playbook shares his script craft ([craft.md](craft.md): the small world, one step of running the rules, the
viewer taking a seat, objections and where the model fails, language) and his style ([style.md](style.md): the small
world).
