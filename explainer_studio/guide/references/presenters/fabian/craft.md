Basis: section-by-section breakdowns of four CGP Grey videos on institutions and historical mechanisms (a whole set of
rules derived from one premise, a table of strata dug downwards, elections run over several rounds, a pie chart growing
one state at a time), Nicky Case's playable explainers, and where viewers got lost in our own videos. Each of his
playbooks (`question-types/`) is built on these techniques; the shared rules
([teaching/rules.md](../../teaching/rules.md)) apply as always.

---

## A. One small world

An episode has only one small world, and all the reasoning happens inside it. The real world enters only as evidence
and in the close.

1. **Actors are seats or groups, not names:** the king, the landowners and generals who hold him up, the peasants,
   voters, patients. 2 to 4 groups, each with one fixed icon and one colour, unchanged for the whole episode and ideally
   for the whole series. A real person appears at most once, in the evidence cell.
2. **One premise:** the one rule that makes the small world hold ("a king needs people holding him up to keep the
   throne", "each voter casts one vote, and the most votes wins"). When you state the premise, say whose model it is
   (the author, the book's title) and that it is simplified.
3. **Rules are derived from the premise:** every rule can be told as "because of the premise, so this". The viewer does
   not have to memorize rules; they can see where each one comes from.
4. **One spine picture:** it grows with the whole episode and is never redrawn (a flowchart of treasure, a bar chart, a
   board written downwards layer by layer). Each step that runs adds one stroke; at the close it is the recap.
5. **One board:** after each step runs, one more line appears on the board with that step's conclusion, as one complete
   sentence (who, what, and so). A viewer who missed a sentence can catch up by reading the board
   ([teaching/visual.md](../../teaching/visual.md#text-the-picture-remembers-the-point-for-the-viewer)).

## B. One step of running the rules

One step is one link (principle 15 in
[teaching/rules.md](../../teaching/rules.md#principles-every-presenter-follows)):

1. A "why?": the question this step answers.
2. A rule, or a condition that changed.
3. An actor does one thing by the rule, and it moves once on the spine picture.
4. The conclusion is said aloud, one more line appears on the board, and a pause follows (`pause_duration_seconds`).

Between steps, the viewer always knows how many steps are left ("Three things. The first…").

## C. The viewer takes a seat

- **A stand-in:** the viewer follows one actor. At the hardest step, switch to "you": "You're sitting on this throne:
  who gets the treasure chest's money first?", "You're this peasant, and the king says anything extra you grow is yours:
  do you believe him?". "You" is any viewer; never quote the answers the user gave in the opening probes.
- **The guess is "what would you do", not "what is the answer":** first let the viewer decide from that seat, then run
  it to show what happens when everyone does that.
- **A new round:** change only one condition, and stop before running to let the viewer think (ask only when they would
  really want to ask; [teaching/rules.md](../../teaching/rules.md#guess-beats)), then run it. Use it only where the
  author really argues this, or where the model itself reasons this way, and say clearly that it is a thought
  experiment.

## D. Objections, and where the model fails

- **First voice the most obvious rebuttal for the viewer,** said by a credible actor in its strongest version, then
  answer it; leave at least one the model does not fully answer.
- **Every episode has a passage on "where the model fails":** at least one real counterexample, acted out with the same
  icons, showing where the model is wrong. Keep apart what the author says, what most scholars agree on, and what is
  still argued over.
- **Give the rival account an equally good-looking small world:** the same way of telling can make two conflicting
  accounts both look inevitable, so what is compared is the two models' predictions, not which sounds better.

## E. Language

- **No metaphors that need translating inside a sentence of reasoning:** talk about the real people and things in the
  small world ("five hundred musketeers are still at his side", not "he still holds the pen"). A metaphor may only be an
  entrance; once used, go back to the actors and rules.
- **Short sentences, one thing per sentence.** Dry humour is fine, growing out of the small world itself (an actor's
  reaction, an absurd result the rules produce), never in the beat that carries the reasoning, and never about death,
  slavery or forced labour.
- **Never say "inevitably", "unavoidably", "the maths decides", "has nothing at all to do with…":** a social science
  model says "under these conditions, tends to…".
- **The close maps the small world's words back onto the sources' terms** (what the book calls it), one word per
  card, so the viewer recognizes them when they read the book later.

## F. Real things get one cell each

- Real countries, eras, people and data each take only one sentence and one picture, as evidence or as a strange
  phenomenon, with no backstory.
- About four or five real places or eras at most per episode, placed after the small world has run. When an episode
  switches between a dozen times and places, the viewer forgets the small world.
- Real numbers are labelled with their source; numbers made up in the small world are labelled "illustrative".

## Self-check (every playbook must pass)

- [ ] Within the first 50 seconds there is a concrete strange phenomenon and a one-sentence main question, and the
      viewer's intuition is said.
- [ ] All reasoning in the episode happens in one small world; actors are seats or groups, with no names; each actor has
      one icon and one colour for the whole episode.
- [ ] When the premise appeared, whose model it is and that it is simplified were said; every rule visibly comes from
      the premise.
- [ ] Every step: one why, one rule, one actor moving once, one conclusion on the board, a pause.
- [ ] At the hardest step the viewer takes a seat and decides; at least one new round, with a pause before running.
- [ ] The most obvious rebuttal is voiced in its strongest version; there is a passage on "where the model fails"; no
      "inevitably" or "unavoidably".
- [ ] No metaphor that needs translating inside a sentence of reasoning; each real case takes one sentence and one
      picture.
- [ ] The close maps the small world's words back onto the sources' terms.
