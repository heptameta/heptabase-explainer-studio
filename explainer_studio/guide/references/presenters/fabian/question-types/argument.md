# Fabian's argument playbook: Test an argument

**The question it answers:** argument, "Why should we believe this claim?"
([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)). Afterwards the viewer can state the claim, the
strongest evidence and the strongest objection, and judge its credibility themselves.

**How it meets the must-haves:** the claim and its rival are each a small world: the same starting point, each with its
own premise and rules. Each world runs once and predicts an outcome for the same real event; then what really happened
is put down, to see which predicted right.

- **The claim says clearly what it asserts and what it does not:** that is its premise, and what it left out of its
  world.
- **Each piece of evidence says what it supports:** every piece of evidence is a place where "the two worlds predict
  differently".
- **The strongest objection:** add one rule to the rival's world and see whether it can explain the evidence too.
- **What is still unknown:** what neither world can explain.

**Use it for:** a claim and a rival that are both causal accounts of how actors behave, with outcomes that really
happened as the evidence: "wealth and poverty are decided by institutions, not geography", "disease made this side win,
not weapons", "this electoral system makes parties fewer and fewer". Both sides can tell a causal chain, and the viewer
must judge which one predicts better. When the evidence itself must be read and understood before you know whom it
supports (chemistry, genes, experimental data, a study's figure), it goes to Theo.

**Versus Theo's "Test an argument":** Theo tells one line: the claim is first built on an example, the rival is stated in
full, then the real data, figures or study results are read item by item; it fits empirical and technical claims (a
paper's finding), where the evidence itself is the thing to be understood. Fabian does not read the evidence first: he
lets the two accounts each run to a prediction, and the evidence is only the moment of the reveal; it fits claims where
both sides argue about causes and the evidence is outcomes that really happened in history.

**Not this one:** several views side by side with no verdict for the viewer (perspectives): Fabian has no playbook for
it, because his way runs out to a winner. Explaining how a mechanism behind the claim works is mechanism
([mechanism.md](mechanism.md)).

How to build the small world, objections and failures, and language are in his script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md));
the picture is in the style ([style.md](../style.md)).

## Only two worlds per episode

The planning rule is that an argument episode tests only one rival, with one test
([teaching/plan.md](../../../teaching/plan.md#planning-how-many-episodes-each-answering-what)). A Fabian episode is one
match: the claim's world against one rival's world, decided by one or two real outcomes the two predict differently.
Other rivals in the sources go to other episodes, or into the plan's `excluded_topics`.

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | Something both sides must explain | A concrete, anomalous real outcome; the main question in one sentence ("Is it A, or B?"); say the viewer's intuition, which is usually the rival's account |
| 8–25% | The rival's world | Build a small world in its strongest version: premise, rules, and whose account it is; run it once, get its prediction, draw it on the spine picture. State it fairly in full; do not say first that it is wrong |
| 25–40% | The claim's world | The same starting point and the same picture, with the author's premise instead; run it once, get its prediction, side by side with the rival's |
| 40–60% | The reveal | A guess: which one really happened? Put down one or two real outcomes the two predict differently, each saying in its first line which question it answers |
| 60–75% | The strongest objection | Add one rule to the rival's world and run it again: can it explain the result just shown? Answer with the single most powerful reason only |
| 75–90% | What is still unknown | What neither world can explain; keep apart what the author says, what most scholars agree on, and what is still argued over |
| 90–100% | Close | Take away one method of testing ("see whether what it predicts matches what really happened later"), which holds for this episode's rival; map the world's words back onto the sources' terms |

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

Each of the two worlds runs once, one real outcome is revealed, one sentence of the strongest objection, one sentence of
what is still unknown.

## Quality bar

After watching, the viewer can say: what each account predicts, which one really happened, what the rival's strongest
reply is, and what is still unknown. Thinking the rival is stupid, or that the author is "certainly right", does not
count.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] The episode has only one rival's world, in its strongest version, with the same starting point and the same
      picture as the claim's world.
- [ ] Every piece of evidence is a place where the two worlds predict differently; the predictions were drawn before the
      evidence.
- [ ] If the viewer is asked anything, it is "which one really happened", and the reveal comes after the question.
- [ ] The strongest objection is answered only once; the testing method the close hands over holds for this episode's
      rival.
- [ ] What neither world can explain was said.
