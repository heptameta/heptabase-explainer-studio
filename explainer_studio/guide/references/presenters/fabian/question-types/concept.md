# Fabian's concept playbook: Separate a concept

Contents: [Only one pair per episode](#only-one-pair-per-episode) · [Skeleton](#skeleton) ·
[When the goal is use](#when-the-goal-is-use) · [Short version](#short-version-6-minutes-or-less) ·
[Quality bar](#quality-bar) · [Self-check](#self-check-in-addition-to-the-craft-self-check)

**The question it answers:** concept, "What is it, and how does it differ from things that look like it?"
([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)). Afterwards the viewer can tell examples from
non-examples and produce one of their own.

**How it meets the must-haves:** the concept is a situation, so put it into a small world: the same groups of actors,
the same picture, change only one condition, and run two worlds.

- **At least two examples:** the same scene run twice, the two examples as different on the surface as possible but with
  that condition the same (drawing for this party, drawing for that party: both are drawing the lines on purpose).
- **A close non-example:** the same scene with only that one condition flipped; the result looks the same as the
  examples, or even more alike (drawn on purpose by proportion and drawn as plain rectangles, coming out identical). When
  there are several conditions, each gets a failure that lacks only it, with the most alike kept for last.
- **The definition comes out of their difference:** the one place where the two worlds differ is the definition; every
  line on the definition card points at that place in the picture.
- **A boundary case the viewer judges:** a real case sitting on the edge of a condition. First put the viewer in the
  seat of an actor in the case and let them decide, then ask "so does it count?", stop and let them judge, and finally
  check it against the conditions one by one.

**Use it for:** a concept about the situation of actors: an institution, a relationship, a kind of competition, a kind
of contract. What separates it from its neighbours is something between the actors (was it on purpose, who recognizes
whom, will they meet again, who follows whom, who can keep whom out), not how it looks. For example "What is
gerrymandering, and how does it differ from ordinary redistricting?", "What is a state, and how does it differ from a
self-governing place?", "How do extractive and inclusive institutions differ?", "What is a repeated game?", "How do
moral hazard and adverse selection differ?". Best when the sources give the definition as a checklist of appearances,
while what really separates it from its neighbours is a situation.

**Versus Theo's, Mason's and Sophie's concept playbooks** (Theo, Fabian, Mason and Sophie all have one; choose by the
shape of the answer, [presenters/README.md](../../README.md)):

- **Theo:** builds the definition step by step from examples and non-examples, and the picture is the concept's own
  structure. When the concept is a structure (a conditional probability, a kind of mathematical object) that remains with
  the actors removed, it goes to Theo.
- **Mason:** sweeps a parameter; the examples, non-examples and boundary run out by themselves from a toy that runs, and
  the definition is a measurable check. When the concept is a behaviour of a system (convergence and divergence,
  overfitting), it goes to Mason. Fabian changes conditions too, but what he changes is a rule or a relationship between
  actors, and what runs out is the actors' choices; and he flips it only once, to show the non-example that looks most
  alike.
- **Sophie:** first lets the viewer give a definition, then breaks and repairs it with cases on the boundary. When the
  concept is a moral or legal distinction (deserving versus being entitled), with boundary cases no world will run out
  an answer for, it goes to Sophie. Fabian's boundary can be judged by applying the conditions; when it cannot, the
  actors disagree about facts (who recognizes, who does not), not about values.

**Not this one:** the viewer wants why this condition causes that result (why meeting again lets trust grow): that is
mechanism ([mechanism.md](mechanism.md)); a concept episode covers it with just one rerun and does not unfold the causal
chain (except when the goal is use; see that section below). Separating right from wrong, should from should not, goes
to Sophie. The viewer wants to learn to do it themselves (how to draw fair districts): that is procedure, which Fabian
has no playbook for ([presenter.md](../presenter.md)).

How to build the small world, one step of running the rules, putting the viewer in a seat, objections and failures, and
language are in his script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md)); the picture is in the style ([style.md](../style.md)).

## Only one pair per episode

One concept per episode, and one neighbour it is most easily confused with. At most four conditions (the models use
two to four); when the sources' list is longer, first find the one or two that really separate it from its
neighbour, and pass over the others in one sentence. Other neighbours in the sources (taming as opposed to
domestication; ordinary infectious disease as opposed to plague) get one sentence and one picture each, or go to other
episodes.

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | A phenomenon you cannot tell apart | A concrete real phenomenon the viewer thinks they can tell at a glance but cannot ("How many countries are there in the world?", "Why do people ride horses and not zebras?"); the main question in one sentence (often: why does this count and that not?); say the viewer's intuitive way of sorting (by looks, against a checklist), and doubt it |
| 8–18% | A fixed scene | The small world: 2 to 4 groups of actors, one premise; say whose definition it is and that it is simplified. Set out a scene that never changes afterwards (15 voters, a ring of countries, a tribe and a herd of animals it can catch); afterwards move only one thing at a time |
| 18–40% | Run out two examples | The same scene run twice, one example each: different on the surface, the same condition. Each run is one step as in [craft.md](../craft.md#b-one-step-of-running-the-rules), the conclusion on the board. At the hardest step the viewer takes a seat and decides ("You draw the lines and want one more seat for your party: how do you draw them?"). When there are several conditions, one beat per condition, each with a failure that lacks only that condition, and one more cell on the board's list |
| 40–55% | The non-example that looks most alike | The same scene with only that one condition flipped; the result looks the same as the examples, or even more alike. The guess is made from the seat: "You're sitting here: what would you do?" (You catch a zebra and put a rope on it: will its family follow?) Pause 2 to 3 seconds, then run; only after it runs ask "so does it count?", side by side with the most alike example, pointing at the one place they differ |
| 55–63% | The definition on the board | Say the definition from the difference between the two worlds: one card, one to four lines, each pointing at that place in the picture; only now give the name; go back and say why the intuitive checklist could not tell them apart |
| 63–78% | Hand the boundary to the viewer | A real case sitting on the edge of a condition, one sentence and one picture (Kosovo, a district drawn on purpose by proportion, the zebra carriage in nineteenth-century London). First the viewer takes the seat of an actor in the case and decides, then ask "so does it count?" and stop to let them judge; then check the conditions one by one. If it meets the conditions but gets the opposite result, repair the definition on the spot; if a condition cannot be judged, say which part is blurred, and who says it counts and who says it does not |
| 78–90% | Where the definition is wrong | At least one real case this definition sorts wrongly or cannot sort; keep apart the author's definition, the one most scholars use, and what is still argued over (for example the recognition theory of the state against the checklist theory) |
| 90–100% | Close | Use the concept to untangle the opening phenomenon; map the small world's words back onto the sources' terms; throw out a case not in the video and let the viewer sort it themselves |

Measured pace (basis: three CGP Grey concept videos and one short concept passage, and one playable Nicky Case
explainer): the scene is always set within the first 8–15%; the non-example that looks most alike always comes in the
second half and gets the most time (up to three tenths of the video); the definition's position ranges from three
tenths of the way in to the last few seconds. Here the definition comes after the non-example and before the boundary,
because the boundary is judged with it. The models almost never let the viewer judge the boundary themselves; that
passage is our addition.

## When the goal is use

The concept's goal is use ([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md#concept-the-other-goal-use)).
**Use it for:** a tool about several groups of actors affecting each other (disruptive innovation, when a commons gets
used up). The scene is fixed; once a round has run out the rule, swap in one real group after another, each running the
most important step in the scene, with the result still growing out of the actors' choices. "Only one pair per episode"
does not apply here. When an earlier mechanism episode already told this causal chain, 16–36% becomes a recall of under
a minute, and the time saved goes to applications.

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | An ending that cannot be explained | A real ending intuition cannot explain (a big company that listened to its customers at every step loses to a small company making something worse); the main question: "Swap in another company: would it lose the same way?" |
| 8–16% | A fixed scene | As in the skeleton above: the small world, 2 to 4 groups of actors, saying whose model it is and that it is simplified; afterwards only the name tags change |
| 16–36% | Run one round, build the tool | Run a round to the end, the hardest decision made by the viewer from a seat; then write the parts and the conditions it needs on one condition card (one to three lines each) on the board, and give the name (the tool's name is the only word mapped back to the sources early); use it to untangle the opening ending |
| 36–64% | Swap in two real groups | The same scene with new name tags (the group's identity, not personal names), running only the most important step: for the first group the viewer guesses from a seat, for the second pause after it is told, then run; then one sentence and one picture of what really happened, said with the name; one group from a field the viewer would not expect |
| 64–80% | The fence: where the model is wrong | A real case where everything seems to match but the model judges wrongly; point out which condition it needs fails; when there is none, say what the model is missing and add a line to the condition card on the spot |
| 80–94% | Your turn to set it up | A real case not seen before: the viewer sets it into the scene, checks it against the condition card and judges; pause, reveal; then one sentence asking them to set up their own company or club |
| 94–100% | Close | Map the world's words back onto the sources' terms; the condition card stays on screen |

- The quality bar is replaced by the one in the use section of
  [teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md#concept-the-other-goal-use). In the self-check keep
  the items about the scene, taking a seat and where the model is wrong; replace those about the non-example, the
  boundary and "the name is filled in only after both worlds have run" with the must-haves of the use row.
- Short version (6 minutes or less): one round, one real group, one sentence of fence, one "your turn"; "your turn" and
  the fence (which is "where it is wrong") can never be cut.
- For example "Disruptive innovation: disk drives (the round) → excavators → a field the viewer would not expect".

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

One scene running one example, one flip of the condition running out the non-example, one definition card, one boundary
question (pause 2 to 3 seconds, one sentence checking the conditions); "where it is wrong" shrinks to one card, but can
never be cut. As a short passage inside another episode (about a minute): one card with the conditions, one example that
meets them, and on the same picture one non-example lacking just one condition; then put it to use straight away.

## Quality bar

Given a case not in the video, the viewer can sort whether it counts and say which condition decides; can say why the
non-example that looks most alike does not count; can produce an example of their own; knows whose definition this is
and where it cannot sort. Remembering only the funny non-example (zebras bite), being unable to say the conditions, or
thinking you can sort by looks or against a checklist, does not count. What this playbook does: the
definition, once given, is used at least twice (once on the non-example, once on the
boundary), and the viewer judges the boundary themselves.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] The examples and the non-example are run out in the same scene: the same groups of actors, the same picture,
      changing one thing; the viewer can say what changed.
- [ ] At least two examples, different on the surface, the same in the condition.
- [ ] The non-example is the one that looks most alike; if the viewer is asked anything, it is "you're sitting here:
      what would you do", with "does it count" following after the run; at the reveal it sits side by side with the
      most alike example.
- [ ] With more than one condition, each has a failure lacking only it, and each failure lacks only one; at most four
      conditions, in the same order on the board as they were told.
- [ ] Every line on the definition card points at the place in the picture where the two worlds differ; the name is
      filled in only after both worlds have run.
- [ ] The viewer judges the boundary first, with a pause, before the conditions are checked one by one; when it meets
      the conditions yet flips, the definition was repaired on the spot.
- [ ] Whose definition this is was said, and at least one place where it cannot sort.
