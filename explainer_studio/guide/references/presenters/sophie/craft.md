Basis: section-by-section breakdowns of four Michael Sandel pieces ("Justice" episodes 1 and 8, the BBC's "The Global
Philosopher", TED) and four Big Questions Dialectic videos (the trolley problem, taxing the rich, free will, the problem
of evil), and where viewers got lost in our own videos. Each of her playbooks (`question-types/`) is built on these
techniques; the shared rules ([teaching/rules.md](../../teaching/rules.md)) apply as always.

---

## A. One set of cases, one change at a time

This is her core. Without it, a discussion falls into monologues taking turns.

1. **Cases come from the sources:** use the book's examples, lawsuits and original texts. A famous classic case (the
   trolley problem) the viewer may have studied; use it only when the sources themselves use it.
2. **One set of cases per episode, three to four,** with the same sketch and the same cast; every view must answer on
   this set.
3. **One change at a time:** each variation removes only one reason, ideally the reason some column has just given
   ("If it's because you push with your own hands, what about pulling a lever instead?"). Before switching, say two
   things: what alone changed this time, and which column it tests (principle 16 in
   [teaching/rules.md](../../teaching/rules.md#principles-every-presenter-follows)).
4. **Real cases come after hypothetical ones:** first draw out the principles with simple cases, then bring on the real
   lawsuit or work; the facts are made clear first and the viewer judges first; the real verdict is revealed last, and
   only as one side's voice.

## B. The beat of a verdict

One case is one beat:

1. **The case:** one sketch, told within 40 seconds.
2. **The verdict card:** two options in the centre of the screen (two choices, so the narration does not double),
   with the narration saying "choose one first; think it through before you go on; you can pause", and a 3-second pause.
   We do not fill it in for the viewer, and we cannot receive their choice.
3. **Narration for both paths:** "If you chose to switch…; if you chose not to switch…", one sentence for each
   choice; the minority path is never left out. Never "most of you" or "most people would", unless there is a source
   ([E](#e-honesty)).
4. **Test this vote with your other vote:** the cell where what the principle says to do does not match "if you chose A
   in the last case, you would probably choose B here" lights up, and the narration asks "If you turned here, what made
   you turn?"
5. **The account:** the position board gains a row: which column held, and which paid a price. Pause
   (`pause_duration_seconds`).

Every stand the viewer takes must be used by the next case; if it will not be used, do not ask for it.

## C. Every view at its strongest

- **At most three columns:** three views, three voices; more and the viewer cannot keep up.
- **One narrator, three voices told apart by spoken openings, with no pretend students and no reliance on tone of voice**
  (the narration has only one voice):
  1. "You might say…": the intuitive version the viewer would say themselves.
  2. "At its strongest, it goes like this…": the upgraded version, its card labelled "strongest version".
  3. "This is how Kant puts it…" plus a passage card: the thinker in person.

  Each sentence comes with a card in that side's colour, so from the colour and the opening the viewer knows who is
  speaking now.
- **Written at its strongest before it enters:** we have no live room, so we cannot wait for someone to give a good
  reason.
- **When caught out, it gets one reply:** after a case hits a column, give it one line of response (concede, revise, or
  point at the other side's cost), then update the account. The last word must not always belong to the critics.
- **Whichever column's turn it is, put it at its strongest, and say so:** "Now I'll make the strongest case for this
  side."

## D. The big picture and the accounts

- **The main question never changes during the episode:** one line at the top, there from start to finish; the close
  answers it, and a new question may come only after the answer, as the hook for the next episode.
- **The position board appears when a verdict is done:** after each case is judged, the board comes out, fills in this
  case's cell (a verdict mark) and leaves again; it is not on screen during details (a reason card, a passage). The
  first time it appears, say what it answers and read one cell on the spot.
- **When stepping back, say "there are this many kinds":** two principles, three objections.
- **No more than two layers of digression within one section:** away from the case too long, the viewer forgets what
  they were judging.

## E. Honesty

- **Never judge the case for the viewer;** if the presenter has a position on the question, say it; if not, say so. On
  "how to argue" she may take a stand (for example "saying everyone just has their own opinion is a dodge, not an
  answer").
- **Say aloud the bias that order creates:** the last one sounds most like the answer; the order of turns is not a
  ranking.
- **Give numbers of people only with a source:** when there is a reliable survey, cite it; when not, only say "if you
  chose…".
- **Read original texts accurately:** translation, source, author; read one sentence to one paragraph, and never use
  the original as an authority that ends the discussion.
- **For argument:** say clearly whose claim it is, and say what is still unknown; do not dress up your own claim as a
  vote.
- **For conceptual change:** conceptual change is settled, so the close must say what people thought later and what it
  cost.

## F. Language and names

- **Column names are plain-language claims** ("one death is better than five", "some things you just must not do"),
  not school names; school names and people's names follow after the plain claim.
- Short sentences, one thing per sentence.
- No jokes on serious subjects: death, violence, slavery, real victims.
- Never pass over the most heated social issues in a minute: the viewer will only try to guess which side the presenter
  is on.
- No internet memes.

## Self-check (every playbook must pass)

- [ ] One set of cases per episode (three to four), from the sources; each variation changes one thing, and before
      switching, what alone changed and which column it tests were said.
- [ ] For every case the viewer judged first (two choices), with a 3-second pause; one sentence for each choice; this
      stand was used by the next case.
- [ ] At most three columns; each column enters in its strongest version; each column replied once after being caught
      out.
- [ ] The main question never changed and the close answered it; the position board appears at seams and steps aside
      during details, with no table-of-contents bar; the board's cells are verdict marks, not sentences, and one cell
      was read the first time it appeared.
- [ ] Every case ended with the account updated: which column held, which paid a price.
- [ ] No case was judged for the viewer; the presenter's position was said (or that there is none); no "most people"
      without a source.
- [ ] Column names are plain-language claims; original texts carry translation, source and author.
