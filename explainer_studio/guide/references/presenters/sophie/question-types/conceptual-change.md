# Sophie's conceptual change playbook: How an intuition was overruled

Contents: [Skeleton](#skeleton) · [Short version](#short-version-6-minutes-or-less) · [Quality bar](#quality-bar) ·
[Self-check](#self-check-in-addition-to-the-craft-self-check)

**The question it answers:** conceptual change, "How did people come to think this way, and where did the old idea fall
short?" ([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)). Afterwards the viewer can say why the old
idea was reasonable, where it broke down, and what the new idea gains and costs. Here the old idea is a moral or legal
intuition still alive in the viewer's head.

**How it meets the must-haves:**

- **The old idea is presented as reasonable:** make it into a case the viewer would judge by it, and let the viewer
  really judge it that way once; then say who thought this way, in which era, and what worse thing it guarded against
  back then. People then were not fools; they judged just as the viewer does now.
- **Where it breaks down is concrete:** change one thing in the same case, ideally the very case that made people change
  their minds in history (a lawsuit, an event). The viewer judges first; the old idea's column disagrees with the
  viewer, and that cell lights up. The breakdown is judged by the viewer themselves.
- **The cost of the new idea is stated too:** the new idea changes one rule on the same case, with its year, people and
  documents. The cost is acted out with a case: bring back a case the viewer judged earlier, or a new one, where the new
  idea gives an answer the viewer is uncomfortable with; then read the strongest line of the opponents of the time.
- **What is settled is historical fact:** the close says what the law, the institutions or most people came to think
  (with sources), not Sophie's verdict. Go only as far as history has settled; the part still argued over gets one
  sentence, pointing to perspectives.

**Use it for:** an old intuition still in the viewer's head that law or institutions have already changed. For example:

- "Why self-defence is no longer judged by who died and who was injured" (China, 2017–2020)
- "Is no resistance consent? How the consent standard for rape changed"
- "Why drawing lots for who gets eaten is not justified" (from the custom of the sea to the 1884 case of Dudley and
  Stephens)
- "How 'birth decides your life' was replaced by 'careers open to talent'"
- "How military service went from a duty to a job" (the United States moving to an all-volunteer force in 1973)

Best when the sources themselves tell how a law, a precedent or an institution changed.

**Versus other presenters' conceptual change playbooks:**

- **Theo** (conceptual change goes to him by default): rebuilds a piece of intellectual history with what people of the
  time could see, with people, a timeline and original texts all doing work on screen; the viewer watches the old
  concept fail on a concrete object. When the old idea is a theory or worldview the viewer does not hold (people once
  thought heat was a fluid), or the viewer needs to see how a discipline itself works, it goes to Theo.
- **Mason:** the old idea is an old method that can be built; its reasonableness, its breakdown and the cost are all run.
- **Sophie:** the old idea is still alive in the viewer's head, and the evidence of its breakdown is a case the viewer
  would judge by the old intuition and that, changed in one place, can no longer be judged that way (the video does not
  know what the viewer judged, so tell both paths with "if you judged…"). History is used only to answer "who else
  judged as you do, and where and when was it overruled".
- Fabian has no playbook for conceptual change.

**Not this one:**

- Still unsettled today (does the difference principle hold up; is consent a matter of the mind or of what is said):
  that is perspectives ([perspectives.md](perspectives.md)) or argument ([argument.md](argument.md)).
- The viewer no longer holds the old intuition (nobody still thinks slavery is acceptable): it goes to Theo. If the
  opening case cannot be judged on the old side, this playbook has no force.
- The protagonist is how one event happened (the course of a reform, of a trial): that is narrative; choose another
  presenter.
- The viewer wants where the line is drawn now, not how it came to be drawn there: that is concept
  ([concept.md](concept.md)).

How to arrange a set of cases, the beat of a verdict, every view at its strongest, the big picture and the accounts,
honesty and language are in her script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md)); the picture is in the style
([style.md](../style.md)). In this playbook the position board is called the **overruling board**: the columns are the
old idea and the new idea (at most three columns when there is a step in between), each column title carrying a year
chip and a person's name; the rows are cases; each cell says how that column judges, with the viewer's judgment as a
hollow mark. Under the board runs a timeline, where the period in which each column was the mainstream is marked as a
stretch in that column's colour.

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–10% | A case the old intuition would judge | The main question in one line, unchanged for the whole episode ("Why isn't it judged this way any more?"); one sketch, told within 40 seconds; the verdict card, a 3-second pause; one sentence for each choice |
| 10–22% | The old idea: who, when, why it was reasonable | The overruling board's first column, one plain sentence ("judge by who died and who was hurt", "no resistance means consent"); turned over: who thought this way and in which era, a short passage of original text; what worse thing it guarded against back then; it works on the opening case |
| 22–45% | One change, and it breaks | One or two variations, each changing one thing, ideally the real case that made people change their minds in history; the viewer judges first; the cell where the first column's answer disagrees with the viewer lights up; say which one place breaks it; the first column replies once (how people back then would have argued) |
| 45–60% | The new idea: one rule changed on the same picture | The second column: one plain sentence, plus the year, people and documents (statute, ruling, guidance), with a short passage read; go back and judge every case again with the second column; with a step in between, do the same, at most three columns |
| 60–80% | The cost | Bring back a case the viewer judged, or a new one: here the new idea gives an answer the viewer is uncomfortable with; what the old idea used to protect and is now given up; read the strongest line of the opponents of the time; update the account |
| 80–100% | What people came to think | The timeline zooms out: when and where each column was the mainstream; say "this is what happened later, not my verdict"; go only as far as history has settled, with one sentence on the part still argued over, pointing to perspectives; back to the old intuition in the viewer's hands: in which kinds of cases it still works, and in which it leads people astray |

Rhythm: one beat per step, 1 to 1.5 minutes each; at most 5 years to remember per episode.

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

- A case for the old intuition, a case changing one thing that breaks it, the new idea's year and documents, one cost.
- "Who, when" and "the cost" can never be cut.

## Quality bar

After watching, the viewer can say:

- why people used to think this way and what it guarded against (and that they themselves judged this way at the
  opening);
- on which case it broke down;
- who changed it, when and where, and into what;
- what the new idea paid;
- next time they meet a new case, recognize that they are using the old intuition again.

Remembering only "people used to be more backward", taking the new idea for the presenter's opinion, or being unable to
say any cost, does not count.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] The viewer really would judge the opening case by the old intuition; if not, switch to Theo.
- [ ] When the old idea entered, who held it, in which era, and what it guarded against back then were said; the people
      of the time were not played as fools, and nothing was mocked.
- [ ] The breaking case comes from the opening case with one change; the viewer judged first, then the first column's
      mismatched cell was shown; the first column replied once.
- [ ] The new idea carries its year, people and documents, with a short passage of original text read; every case was
      judged again with the new column.
- [ ] The cost is acted out with a case (ideally one the viewer judged), and the opponents' strongest line was read.
- [ ] The close states historical fact (with sources), not "so you should"; it goes only as far as history has
      settled, pointing the disputed part to perspectives; where and in which year it was settled is made clear.
- [ ] At most three columns per episode, and no more than 5 years to remember.
