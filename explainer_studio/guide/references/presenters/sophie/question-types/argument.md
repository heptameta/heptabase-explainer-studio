# Sophie's argument playbook: Try a claim

**The question it answers:** argument, "Why should we believe this claim?"
([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)). Afterwards the viewer can state:

- the claim;
- the strongest evidence and the strongest objection;
- how credible they themselves judge it to be.

**How it meets the must-haves:**

- **The claim says clearly what it asserts and what it does not:** first ask the viewer to judge a case, then state the
  claim (often the very principle behind that judgment), and mark its boundary with a case that changes one thing.
- **Each piece of evidence says what it supports:** each piece of evidence is preferably a variation of the same set of
  cases, with a passage of original text when needed. The viewer judges first, then you say which part of the claim it
  supports, recorded on the board. The board has two columns, "the claim" and "the strongest objection"; one row per
  case, with passage cards hung on their rows.
- **The strongest objection is told:** she puts it at its heaviest, reading the other side's own words.
- **What is still unknown is said:** neither model does this; Sophie must.

**Use it for:** normative or interpretive claims, where the evidence is judgments of cases and original texts, not data
and experiments. For example:

- "Does Rawls's difference principle hold up?"
- "Is walking essential to golf?" (a lawsuit)
- "Is the 'cold' in this poem about the weather or a state of mind?" (whether a reading holds up)
- "Is the justice of the *Republic* a defence of rule by the few?" (whether a reading holds up)

**Versus other presenters' argument playbooks:**

- **Theo:** when the evidence needs data, experiments or studies to be read and understood (chemistry, genes,
  statistics).
- **Fabian:** when the claim and its rival are both causal accounts of how actors behave, with outcomes that really
  happened as the evidence.
- **Sophie:** the evidence is your judgments of cases and original texts. She has the viewer judge before every piece
  of evidence, so the step of judging the claim's credibility themselves is something the viewer has been doing all
  along.

**Not this one:**

- Several views side by side with no verdict for the viewer is perspectives ([perspectives.md](perspectives.md)).
- A claim that is a causal account of "what happened and why": choose another presenter
  ([presenters/README.md](../../README.md)).

How to arrange a set of cases, the beat of a verdict, every view at its strongest, the big picture and the accounts,
honesty and language are in her script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md)); the picture is in the style
([style.md](../style.md)).

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | A case, and the claim | A case the viewer judges first; then state the claim (often the very principle behind that judgment), and who made it |
| 8–15% | The boundary | A case that changes one thing, saying what the claim asserts and what it does not |
| 15–55% | Evidence: two or three pieces | Each is one beat: a case or a passage of original text, the viewer judges first, then say which part of the claim it supports; one row on the board |
| 55–80% | The strongest objection | Ordered from weak to strong, the strongest put at its heaviest, with its own words read; test it on the same set of cases; the claim replies once |
| 80–90% | What is still unknown | Which cases neither side can settle, and what is still argued over |
| 90–100% | Close | The board zooms out: what the claim survived, and where it paid a price; how credible the presenter finds it (with reasons), and the viewer judges for themselves |

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

- One piece of evidence, one strongest objection.
- "What is still unknown" shrinks to one sentence, but can never be cut.

## Quality bar

After watching, the viewer can say:

- the claim and its boundary;
- which case or passage is the strongest evidence, and which the strongest objection;
- how far they believe it, and why.

Remembering only "the author says…", or thinking the claim has been proven, does not count.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] The opening has one plain-language claim, and a case marks its boundary.
- [ ] Before every piece of evidence the viewer judged first; for each, which part it supports was said.
- [ ] The strongest objection was put at its heaviest, with its own words read; the claim replied once.
- [ ] What is still unknown was said.
