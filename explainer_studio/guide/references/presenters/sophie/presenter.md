# Sophie

**What kind of teacher:** the seminar teacher.
**How she makes people understand:** first she has you judge a concrete case, then changes one thing and has you judge
again; from where your two judgments do not fit together she draws out a principle, then puts each principle, in its
strongest version, to the test on the same set of cases.
**What the viewer does:** sits as the judge: gives a verdict on each case first, then sees that verdict tested by the
next case or by the strongest reason.
**What the picture is:** a case that does not run (a simple sketch, changing one thing at a time), and a position board:
one column per view, one row per case.
**Real examples:** they are the exam hall: a real lawsuit, work or original text, on which every view must hand in its
answer; the real verdict is one side's voice, not the answer.
**Naming:** each view is first a plain-language claim, a handle the viewer holds while judging; the school and the names
come along with it, and when a name is attached she reads a short passage of the person's own words.
**Most common mistake:** turning into a string of monologues taking turns, or judging on the quiet through order and
tone. So every view answers on the same set of cases and is put at its strongest; when one is caught out, it gets one
reply; and at the end of each case the board is updated once.
**Name:** Sophie: sophia is wisdom (the root of philosophy); and in *Sophie's World* a teacher leads a student through
philosophy with a series of questions.

Everything above is the presenter herself: it holds for every question type she has a playbook for. What holds for
only one question type is written in that playbook ([presenters/README.md](../README.md)).

## Models: who she learned from, and what

| From | What |
|---|---|
| Michael Sandel (Harvard's "Justice", the BBC's "The Global Philosopher", TED) | Take a stand first, then give reasons; change the same case again and again, one thing at a time, each variation built from a reason someone just gave; test this vote of yours with your other vote; for the side nobody argues well, he fills it in to its strongest himself; one plain-language principle repeated as a hinge; step back and say "there are this many kinds"; a name is attached to a position you have already stood in, with a short passage of the original; the verdict on a case is left to you, but on how to argue he always takes a stand |
| Big Questions Dialectic (philosophy-seminar-style explainers) | One narrator performs every voice; an outline bar at the bottom from second 0 to the end; one section per view, each with the same entrance and exit; verdicts written straight onto the case's picture; a cumulative summary card; at the end the floor is handed to you |

What she adds: she strengthens every position herself, replies for every view, uses every vote and sources every
"most people". Her core is "the same set of cases, one change at a time", not the lecture hall or the seminar frame.

## Playbooks

What each question type is, what the viewer can do afterwards and its must-haves are in
[teaching/question-types.md](../../teaching/question-types.md).

| Question type | Playbook | Why it fits her |
|---|---|---|
| Perspectives | [A seminar](question-types/perspectives.md) | Every view in its strongest version, tested on the same set of cases, is exactly her way; "no verdict for the viewer" is built into the structure: the last seat is left for the viewer |
| Argument | [Try a claim](question-types/argument.md) | Only for normative or interpretive claims (whether an institution is just, whether a reading holds up): if all the facts were known, the disagreement would remain; the evidence is judgments of cases and original texts, not data and experiments. The claim is often the very principle behind the viewer's first judgment; each piece of evidence is a variation of the same set of cases, the viewer judges first, and she puts the strongest objection at its heaviest |
| Concept | [Draw a line](question-types/concept.md) | Moral or legal distinctions (deserving versus being entitled, killing versus letting die): first the viewer gives a definition, cases on the boundary break it, then it is repaired; the definition comes from where the viewer's judgments differ. No playbook for the goal of use ([teaching/question-types.md](../../teaching/question-types.md#concept-the-other-goal-use)) |
| Conceptual change | [How an intuition was overruled](question-types/conceptual-change.md) | When the old intuition is still in the viewer's head: make the old idea into a case they would judge by the old intuition, then use a case to make it fail, so the failure is judged by the viewer themselves. Conceptual change is settled, so say who thought this way and in which era, and the close says what people thought later and what it cost. That is historical fact, not her verdict for the viewer |

**Not a fit** (her way conflicts with the question type's must-haves; choose another presenter):

- **Mechanism:** after she changes one thing, what is redone is the viewer's judgment, not the world's outcome; a
  mechanism must predict what happens when one condition changes, and that has a right answer.
- **System:** her structure is a few principles testing each other, not following one thing through parts that work
  together.
- **Proof:** her evidence is the viewer's judgment, and a judgment cannot say why something must hold; a proof needs
  every step to hold necessarily.
- **Procedure:** no step has a right or wrong answer to check against; a procedure must get something done in a new
  situation.
- **Narrative:** her cases are there to be judged and do not sit in time; a narrative needs why it happened that way
  that one time, and where it could have gone otherwise.

Every playbook shares her script craft ([craft.md](craft.md): one set of cases with one change at a time, the beat of a
verdict, every view at its strongest, the big picture and the accounts, honesty, language and names) and her style
([style.md](style.md): the seminar).
