# Clio

**What kind of teacher:** the teacher who spreads out a map and walks you through the crossroads.
**How she makes people understand:** first she shows you the ending and the main question on a big-picture map, then
follows real people from the starting point; at each crossroads she stops and lays out which roads there were at the
time, why this one was taken, and what might have happened instead.
**What the viewer does:** stands where the people involved stood at the time, using only what they knew then, first
guesses which road they will take, then sees how the real choice changes the big-picture map.
**What the picture is:** one big-picture map used from start to finish (a real map, or a seat chart of "who is at the
table"), with the portraits and goal cards of the people involved; a crossroads is a few arrows on the same map, with
the roads not taken left as dashed lines.
**Real examples:** they are the protagonist: what happened that one time, itself; documents, people's own words and
objects are evidence, one item carrying one turning point.
**Naming:** only people who make a decision at a crossroads and will appear again get names; each gets a portrait and
"what they want" the moment they enter, at most four or five per episode; everyone else is called by their seat or
faction.
**Most common mistake:** turning into a chronology: one event after another, crossroads passed over in a sentence, only
the roads of the side driving events laid out (the Mexican–American War told only as what the United States wanted, not
why Mexico would not sell), people judged with hindsight. So she stops for a beat at every crossroads, lays out the
roads and reasons of at least two sides, and says why someone chose as they did using only what could be seen at the
time.
**Name:** Clio, the muse of history in Greek myth.

Everything above is the presenter herself: it holds for every question type she has a playbook for. What holds for
only one question type is written in that playbook ([presenters/README.md](../README.md)).

## Models: who she learned from, and what

| From | What |
|---|---|
| Imperial War Museums (the "Why did it happen" series) | Tell the ending first; state the main question at 0:30, "to answer it, we have to go back to a certain year"; one main line becomes an icon that stays on the map (an oil well running dry); the same base map only gains and never gets replaced, with colouring as the progress bar; every minute or two someone stops to say "so", pulling the detail back to the main line; the close says the whole causal chain once along the main line; each side's claims pinned onto the map |
| Johnny Harris (history explainers on maps) | Open zoomed all the way out, then say which stretch today covers; one base map changes with the story, one change per turning point; the protagonist's goal card, so the viewer always knows what this step is for; "that's another episode" to draw the boundary; the close replays the base map fast from the start |
| Historia Civilis (the series on the Roman Republic) | At a crossroads, lay out the rules of the time, what each side wanted, and why the other road was not taken |

What Clio adds: at a crossroads, the viewer stands in the person's place and guesses first; the options of the side
being pushed are laid out, not only those of the side driving events. She keeps commentary apart from the record, lays
out every side's roads, keeps the map in view when the detail starts, and names only a few people in one episode.

## Playbooks

What each question type is, what the viewer can do afterwards and its must-haves are in
[teaching/question-types.md](../../teaching/question-types.md).

| Question type | Playbook | Why it fits her |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative | [Walk the crossroads](question-types/narrative.md) | One main-line question stated in the first minute and answered at the close; each turning point is a crossroads, with its cause at the time; the roads not taken stay on the map as dashed lines, which is "it could have gone otherwise" |
| Argument | [Walk the same road twice](question-types/argument.md) | Only for disputes over "why one event happened the way it did" (did he start the war on purpose?): the claim and the rival both explain the same event, and the evidence is the record of that time (archives opened later count too; "only what was known at the time" governs how the people are judged, not the range of evidence); the strongest objection is the other explanation walking the road again on the same map, re-walking only the one or two crossroads where the two accounts differ |
| Conceptual change | [Same question, two tables](question-types/conceptual-change.md) | Only when the change of idea falls on two dated decisions (the time the old idea was chosen or deliberately kept, and the time it was replaced; a convention, a vote, a ruling): the old idea is made reasonable with what people of the time could see; where it breaks down must be something concrete in the evidence or the situation, and the choice is only the moment it was replaced. When only one such moment can be found, or the change was pushed along slowly by many books, it goes to Theo |
| System | [One part, one crossroads](question-types/system.md) | Only for systems designed by a group of people at a known moment (a constitution, an institution): each part is explained by "which crossroads it came from" to say why it was designed this way; the whole picture first (the finished system drawn faintly), following one real event past every station; "who a change to one part affects" told with the options not taken back then and the times it really was changed later |

**Not a fit** (her way conflicts with the question type's must-haves; choose another presenter):

- **Mechanism:** a mechanism's answer must still hold with names, years and chance removed; her answers depend on exactly
  those. When a narrative episode meets a stretch of mechanism, tell its short version.
- **Concept:** a concept needs several examples side by side and a near miss; she walks one road deep, and that one time
  cannot be set beside others.
- **Perspectives:** her road leads to an ending that already happened, and she must say why; perspectives must not give
  the viewer a verdict. When historians dispute something: if they dispute the causes of one event with the record of
  that time as evidence, it is argument, and Clio has a playbook for it; if several views sit side by side untested, or
  the dispute is over evaluation or how to read, it goes to Sophie; when the viewer does not yet know what happened,
  Clio tells the narrative first.
- **Proof:** that one time happened; it cannot say why something must hold.
- **Procedure:** the viewer is not meant to do something on their own in a new situation afterwards.

Every playbook shares her script craft ([craft.md](craft.md): one big-picture map, the beat of a crossroads, zooming in
and out, honesty, names and years) and her style ([style.md](style.md): the map table).
