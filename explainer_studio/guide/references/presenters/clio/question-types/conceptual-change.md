# Clio's conceptual change playbook: Same question, two tables

Contents: [Skeleton](#skeleton) · [Short version](#short-version-6-minutes-or-less) · [Quality bar](#quality-bar) ·
[Self-check](#self-check-in-addition-to-the-craft-self-check)

**The question it answers:** conceptual change, "How did people come to think this way, and where did the old idea fall
short?" ([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)), only when the change of idea falls on two
dated decisions: the time the old idea was chosen (or deliberately kept), and the time it was replaced. Afterwards the
viewer can say why the old idea was reasonable, where it broke down, and what the new idea gains and costs.

**How it meets the must-haves:**

- **The old idea is presented as reasonable:** the old idea too was chosen (or deliberately kept) at a table. Go back
  to that time: the sides' goal cards on the seat chart, what they had in hand then, which roads were on the table; the
  viewer guesses first (the title has already said they chose the old idea, so what they guess is which reason
  mattered most to them, or what the other side expected), then sees why they chose as they did; the road not chosen
  stays as a dashed line.
- **Where it breaks down is concrete:** after the old idea took effect, each time it failed is one stroke on the
  big-picture map, each stroke carried by a document or a number; every two or three strokes get one "so", gathered into
  one cause.
- **The cost of the new idea is stated too:** ask the same question again at the second table, on the same seat chart:
  who changed seats, what they now had in hand; the options side by side (the first table's dashed line is often among
  them); guess first (who will back which option, and why), then see which was chosen and what the new idea gains; the
  losing side's goal cards stay: what they feared, what compensation they won, what really happened afterwards.

**Use it for:** an idea (an institution, a rule, a classification) that was first chosen or deliberately kept at one
table, and later replaced at another, both times with people and dates; the second table can be a different group (a
court, a congress). For example:

- "How did Americans go from 'a league of thirteen states' to 'one nation'?"
- "Why did the British Army lift its ban on gay service members in 2000, and why not in 1967?"
- "How did China move from the 'two whatevers' to 'practice is the sole criterion of truth'?"

**Versus other presenters' conceptual change playbooks:**

- **Theo** (the default for conceptual change): a piece of intellectual history, the old concept failing on a concrete
  object; the change often took decades and many books. When only one table can be found, or none, it goes to Theo.
- **Sophie:** the old idea is a moral or legal intuition still in the viewer's head, and the evidence of its breakdown is
  the viewer's own judgment of a case. On the same topic (the ban on gay service members), asking "on what grounds did
  the court say the old intuition fails?" goes to Sophie; asking "why 2000, and why not 1967?" goes to Clio (the
  conceptual change row of [presenters/README.md](../../README.md#the-same-question-type-several-presenters-playbooks):
  when both fit, decide by how the question is asked).
- **Mason:** the old idea is an old method that can be built, run until it breaks.
- **Clio:** the change of idea is two dated decisions; the viewer stands beside both tables, compares "what they now had
  in hand", sees why the idea changed, and also sees the road that was struck out come back later.

**Not this one:**

- The change drifted slowly over decades, with no table to be found (how nationalism formed; how people before
  partition came to see each other only through religion): it goes to Theo; when the sources hide one decision, that
  decision gets a short version inside Theo's episode.
- The protagonist is how that one decision was reached (how the constitutional convention ran), not the idea: that is
  narrative ([narrative.md](narrative.md)). When unsure, ask: does the viewer take away "why the idea changed"
  (conceptual change), or "how those few days of negotiation went" (narrative)?
- The new idea is still unsettled and argued over today: that is perspectives, and goes to Sophie.

How to draw the big-picture map, the beat of a crossroads, zooming in and out, honesty, and names and years are in her
script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md)); the picture is in the style ([style.md](../style.md)). In this playbook the
big-picture map must draw the state of the idea: the distances between the sides on a map (thirteen states apart or
pieced together), the lines on a seat chart (a league is dashed, a federation solid), or, without geography, a rule card
in the middle of the table ("Congress may not tax"), turned over when replaced. Both tables use the same seat chart; the
second time only the people and the goal cards change (with `swap_seat` when a different group takes over), so the
viewer can compare the difference.

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–6% | The ending first | Something visible in today's new idea (a word, a clause) → it used to be exactly the opposite → the main question in one sentence ("Why did they first choose A, and later switch to B?") → the big-picture map appears for the first time, with the idea's destination drawn faintly; the years of the two tables marked on the time bar |
| 6–25% | The first table: the old idea is chosen | The weight goes on what they had just been through: that is where the old idea's reasons came from (a distant government had just imposed taxes by force, so they did not want a centre that could tax), acted out, not just said in a sentence; the seat chart and each side's goal cards; two or three arrows on the table and one guess beat are enough (guess which reason mattered most to them, a 3-second pause); the old idea is chosen, and why it was reasonable is said using only what was known at the time; the road not chosen stays as a dashed line, "remember this one: it will come back" |
| 25–50% | Breaking down, stroke by stroke | Three to five strokes, each a mark on the big-picture map, carried by a document or a number; every two or three strokes get one "so"; who stood by and saw the problem (the new idea's proposer enters here, with a goal card) |
| 50–75% | The second table: the same question again | The same seat chart: who changed seats, what they now had in hand; the options side by side, the first table's dashed line lighting up; guess first who backs which; which was chosen and why, and what the new idea gains; this is where the whole road turns, and say so |
| 75–90% | The new idea's cost | The losing side's goal cards stay: what they feared, what compensation they won; what really happened afterwards is written on the map; evaluations go on opinion cards; which part of the old idea was kept |
| 90–100% | Close | The two tables replayed side by side, comparing "what they now had in hand"; one paragraph answering the main question; the dashed lines light up again, one beat each ([craft.md](../craft.md#b-the-beat-of-a-crossroads), step 5); one sentence on where this idea stands today: go only as far as history has settled (what is still argued over afterwards, say is still argued over) |

Measured pace (a map explainer on why America's first constitution failed, about 18 minutes once the ads and the first
half on constitutional design are removed): the opening 1 minute 35 seconds (8%), the old idea and its reasons 3 minutes
35 seconds (19%), the mess 4 minutes (21%), reaching the second table and the crossroads 5 minutes 10 seconds (28%), the
cost 1 minute 30 seconds (8%, and only appearing 30 minutes into the original video); a 2-minute stretch of war in the
middle is a side branch. Another, on the British Army's ban on gay service members, scatters five years across 20
minutes, and its longest stretch (an interview of nearly 4 minutes) has no year at all. Clio: the first table can never
be skipped, but its weight is in the causes before it: the model uses the British tax passage plus one sentence, "the
weaker the centre the better", to give the old idea's reasons, and that is enough; the options on the table need only
one beat. The cost must come right after the second table.

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

- One beat for each table, only two strokes of breakdown, the cost as one sentence plus the losing side's goal card.
- The first table and the cost can never be cut.

## Quality bar

After watching, the viewer can say:

- when the old idea was first chosen, what the people at the table had in hand, and why choosing it was reasonable;
- two concrete things that made it break down;
- what the second table had in addition, what it chose, and whether the road struck out the first time came back;
- what the new idea gained, and who paid what for it.

Remembering only "people used to think wrongly and later got it right", or only a string of events, does not count.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] Two dated tables can be found (the first may be the time the old idea was deliberately kept); when only one can be
      found, or the change drifted, switch to Theo.
- [ ] The first table: what they had just been through was acted out (the source of the old idea's reasons); goal
      cards, two or three roads, a guess first, why the old idea was reasonable said using only what was known at the
      time; the road not chosen stays as a dashed line.
- [ ] Each stroke of breakdown is a mark on the big-picture map, carried by a document or a number, gathered into a
      "so".
- [ ] The second table uses the same seat chart, saying who changed seats and what they now had in hand; the first
      table's dashed line is accounted for (it came back, or why it did not).
- [ ] The losing side's roads and reasons were laid out; the new idea's cost comes right after the choice, with facts on
      the map and evaluations on opinion cards.
- [ ] The state of the idea is drawn on the big-picture map throughout, so the viewer can always see which idea holds
      now.
