# Clio's argument playbook: Walk the same road twice

Contents: [Skeleton](#skeleton) · [Short version](#short-version-6-minutes-or-less) · [Quality bar](#quality-bar) ·
[Self-check](#self-check-in-addition-to-the-craft-self-check)

**The question it answers:** argument, "Why should we believe this claim?"
([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)), only when the claim and its rival both explain why
the same event happened the way it did. Afterwards the viewer can state the claim, the strongest evidence and the
strongest objection, and judge its credibility themselves.

**How it meets the must-haves:**

- **The claim says clearly what it asserts and what it does not:** the claim is the author's account in the sources
  (when the sources take no side, the mainstream account); the rival is the strongest other one; the first walk follows
  the claim. The opening circles on the big-picture map the crossroads both accounts explain (at most two), with two
  explanation cards of one sentence each, marked with whose account it is; the road both sides agree on is drawn solid
  first, labelled "not in dispute"; the back of each explanation card says what it does not assert.
- **Each piece of evidence says what it supports:** every piece of evidence is the record of that time (archives opened
  later and post-war interrogations count too; "only what was known at the time" governs how the people are judged, not
  the range of evidence), pinned on the big-picture map at the day and the table it speaks of (what was said afterwards
  has a border marked with the year it was said). The viewer first stands in the person's place and guesses, then sees
  what the record says, and how each explanation card reads it (what is in dispute is exactly what was in the person's
  mind, so the record cannot reveal the answer); one sentence says which part of which card it supports more, and that
  card gets a mark. What was written at the time and what was said afterwards are told apart by two kinds of border
  (`pin_evidence`, `get_passage`), and what was said afterwards is discounted.
- **The strongest objection is told:** the other account, in its strongest version, re-walks on the same map the one or
  two crossroads where they differ, explaining the same evidence. Each account draws a counterfactual line ("if there
  had been no …, what would have happened"), side by side at the same crossroads; counterfactual lines are dotted and
  labelled "if there had been no ___", kept apart from the roads the people themselves did not take (dashed); the claim
  replies once.
- **What is still unknown is said:** nobody has walked either counterfactual line; which records do not exist, which
  words were said afterwards; the decision was made by a group, each with possibly different reasons, so no one can
  say what "they" feared most; what is broadly agreed (often "both mattered") is written on the map, and what is still
  argued over on opinion cards. A third account (for example "it took both together") goes in this section; only two
  accounts fight in one episode.

**Use it for:** an event with two accounts of its cause, where both sides broadly agree on what happened and argue over
why the people of that time chose as they did. For example:

- "Did Japan surrender in August 1945 because of the atomic bombs, or because the Soviet Union entered the war?"
- "Did Hitler attack the Soviet Union mainly because of a long-held ideological plan, or because the resource squeeze of
  1940 forced him?"
- "Did Polk start the Mexican–American War on purpose?"

**Versus other presenters' argument playbooks:**

- **Fabian:** the claim is a generalization that applies to many cases (will nuclear weapons force a country to
  surrender?), and the evidence is many real outcomes.
- **Theo:** the evidence itself must be read and understood (the statistics of a bombing survey, a study's figure).
- **Sophie:** the facts are known, and the dispute is over evaluation or how to read (normative or interpretive
  arguments); "was dropping the atomic bomb right?" is perspectives, and goes to her too.
- **Mason:** the claim can be run on a small toy. History cannot be run, so there is no overlap.
- **Clio:** one event, with the record of that time as evidence; the two accounts dispute the dashed road not taken at
  the same crossroads, and at that crossroads the viewer first stands in the person's place and guesses, then sees the
  record.

**Not this one:**

- The viewer does not yet know what happened that time: first schedule a narrative episode ([narrative.md](narrative.md)),
  and open this episode with a one-minute replay of that road. If you are not sure whether they know it, look at the
  audience probes: if they had not heard of the event, schedule the narrative first.
- The question asks should or should not, right or wrong: that is perspectives or a normative argument, and goes to
  Sophie.
- Historians' views side by side, untested, without letting the viewer judge: that is perspectives, and goes to Sophie.
- The title is a choice of two, but the answer needs the whole course of events told (take away the two accounts, and
  the rest still needs ten minutes or more): it is really answering a narrative; use [narrative.md](narrative.md), with
  the dispute shrunk to a short version at the close.
- The dispute is over decades of policy or structure ("divide and rule"), with no table where everyone agrees on the
  road before it: it goes to Sophie for the several views, or Theo to read the evidence.

How to draw the big-picture map, the beat of a crossroads, zooming in and out, honesty, and names and years are in her
script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md)); the picture is in the style ([style.md](../style.md)). In this playbook the
big-picture map draws the table where the decision was made (a seat chart) and the time bar of those few days;
battlefields and cities are details zoomed in on. There are two explanation cards (`get_explanation_card`), on either
side of the big-picture map, each with one colour for the whole episode, not any side's colour (a "Soviet entry" card in
Soviet red looks like the Soviets' own account). After each piece of evidence is read, a pin goes on the card it
supports more, and one card can receive different parts of the same piece of evidence. The historians on the
explanation cards do not count towards the episode's four or five named people; when the table has many people, name
no more than five, and call the others by their seat (the navy, the general staff). Turning points on the shared road
are not crossroads: in the fast-forward they get one sentence and no beat.

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–6% | The ending and the two accounts | A dated picture → the ending → "Why? There are two accounts" → two explanation cards, one sentence each, marked with whose account → circle on the big-picture map the crossroads they both explain → the main question in one sentence ("Was it A or B that made ___ happen?") |
| 6–18% | The road both sides agree on | One minute of replay when an earlier narrative episode exists; without one, at most three minutes, and beyond that a narrative episode should be scheduled first. The big-picture map fast-forwards from the start to just before the disputed crossroads (turning points on the way get one sentence, no beat): the people at the table, their goal cards, a few days on the time bar; this road is drawn solid, labelled "both sides agree"; the explanation cards turn over to say what each does not assert |
| 18–50% | Evidence: zoom in at the disputed crossroads | Two to four pieces of evidence, one beat each: zoom in to that day, that table → guess first ("You are ___, at ___, and this is all you know: what would you say first?", "In this order, which item do you think he mentions first?", "You're the other side: what do you expect him to do?", a 3-second pause) → see the record (a quotation card, written at the time or said afterwards, with different borders) → how each explanation card reads it, which part of which card it supports more, and a pin → zoom out |
| 50–75% | The other account walks it | Switch to the other explanation card, in its strongest version, re-walking only the one or two crossroads where they differ: how it explains each piece of evidence just shown; when both sides use the same evidence, say so outright; two dashed lines drawn side by side at that crossroads; the claim replies once |
| 75–88% | What is still unknown | Nobody has walked either counterfactual line; missing records, words said afterwards, a decision made by a group; what is broadly agreed written on the map, what is still argued over on opinion cards |
| 88–100% | Close | The big-picture map replays, with the evidence on the two explanation cards side by side; which side the author or presenter finds more credible goes on an opinion card, with reasons; back to the main question: say what each side rests on and how far the broad consensus goes, and ask the viewer to judge; one sentence: the same kind of dispute exists over other events, for another episode |

Measured pace (a history explainer that told each school's evidence once without a verdict, its half on why Japan
surrendered): the two accounts at the opening 1 minute 20 seconds, the shared facts 5 minutes 20 seconds, the orthodox
school 2 minutes 10 seconds, the revisionists 3 minutes 50 seconds, what-ifs 2 minutes 20 seconds, the limits of the
sources about a minute; roughly one piece of evidence every 40 to 60 seconds, quotation cards held for 10 to 20 seconds.
Clio: one piece of evidence per beat, 1 to 1.5 minutes per beat (with the guess and the account added); the shared road:
one minute when an earlier narrative episode exists, at most three minutes without.

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

- Two explanation cards, one minute of shared road, one piece of evidence per side, two dashed lines side by side, one
  sentence of what is still unknown.
- The guess first may be kept only once; the dashed lines side by side and "what is still unknown" can never be cut.

## Quality bar

After watching, the viewer can say:

- what each account asserts and what it does not;
- each side's strongest piece of evidence, and whether it was written at the time or said afterwards;
- where each dashed line leads, and why nobody can be sure;
- which side they believe more, and because of which piece of evidence.

Remembering only what happened, or only "historians still argue about it", does not count.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] The two accounts answer the same question and both explain the same crossroads on the big-picture map.
- [ ] The road both sides agree on was drawn solid first and walked in about two minutes; this episode did not turn into
      a narrative episode.
- [ ] The big-picture map draws the table where the decision was made and those few days; battlefields appear only when
      zoomed in on.
- [ ] Before every piece of evidence there was a guess; after the reveal it was pinned to an explanation card, saying
      which part it supports; what was written at the time and what was said afterwards have different borders.
- [ ] The other account entered in its strongest version and re-walked only the crossroads in dispute; when both sides
      used the same evidence, that was said outright.
- [ ] The two dashed lines are drawn side by side, saying why nobody can be sure where they lead.
- [ ] The presenter's judgment is on an opinion card, with reasons; what is broadly agreed and what is still argued over
      are kept apart.
