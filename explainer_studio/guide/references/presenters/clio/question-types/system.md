# Clio's system playbook: One part, one crossroads

Contents: [Skeleton](#skeleton) · [Short version](#short-version-6-minutes-or-less) · [Quality bar](#quality-bar) ·
[Self-check](#self-check-in-addition-to-the-craft-self-check)

**The question it answers:** system, "How do the parts of this system work together?"
([teaching/question-types.md](../../../teaching/question-types.md)), only for systems designed by a group of people at a
known moment. Afterwards the viewer can, given a part, say what it is responsible for, why it was designed this way back
then, and whom a change to it would affect.

**How it meets the must-haves:**

- **The whole picture first:** tell the ending first: the whole finished system drawn faintly on the big-picture map
  (the parts as rows of seats, with who sits there, how they get there and whom they can block written beside them);
  then go back to the table where it was designed, and at each crossroads fill one part in solid.
- **Follow one concrete thing through every part:** once the system is filled in, take one real thing (a real bill, a
  real crisis, a real case) from start to finish, lighting up each part as it passes, saying what that part did and
  which crossroads left it there.
- **Say who a change to one part affects:** the option not chosen at each crossroads stays as a dashed version of that
  part; pick the most important one, swap the dashed version in, let the same real thing walk through again, and say who
  gains and who loses (using the consequences people feared back then, or numbers historians have worked out); for parts
  that really were changed later (an amendment, an institution added afterwards), say whom the change affected.

**Use it for:** a system designed in a certain year, at a certain table (when it took several rounds of negotiation, the
same seat chart with each round marked on the time bar), where the viewer wants to know what each part does and why it
looks the way it does. For example:

- "Why was the US Constitution designed this way: why two chambers, why is the president not directly elected?"
- "Why is NATO's Article 5 worded so vaguely?"
- "Why does the UN Security Council have five vetoes?"
- "Why does the Constitution of the Republic of China have five branches?"

**Versus other presenters' system playbooks:**

- **Theo:** components behave as designed, and why each station is designed this way can be explained by reasoning (a
  program, a timetable), whatever other options there were back then.
- **Fabian:** the parts are actors who make choices; change one rule and see how everyone changes what they do (abolish
  winner-takes-all, and which states candidates campaign in changes).
- **Mason:** all the components run together, and one piece of data is followed past every station.
- **Clio:** each part's "why" is a crossroads: that year, that table, which options, who wanted which; "who a change
  affects" is told with the options not chosen and the times it really was changed later. On the same topic (the
  electoral college), asking how it makes candidates campaign today goes to Fabian; asking why in 1787 neither Congress
  nor the people were allowed to choose directly goes to Clio.

**Not this one:**

- A system with no moment of design, one that grew slowly (Britain's unwritten constitution, a market): it goes to Theo,
  Fabian or Mason.
- The protagonist is how that table got its result, who persuaded whom (the course of the constitutional convention):
  that is narrative ([narrative.md](narrative.md)). When unsure, ask: does the viewer take away "given a part, say what
  it does and why it was designed this way" (system), or "how it was negotiated that time" (narrative)?
- The question asks about only one part (the Security Council veto): split it into its sub-parts (who holds it, how far
  it reaches, how it can be changed); if you cannot get three, it is narrative.
- The protagonist is how an idea behind the design was replaced (a strong centre or a weak one): that is conceptual
  change ([conceptual-change.md](conceptual-change.md)).
- The viewer wants how it works today and how people would game a rule change: give it to Fabian, Theo or Mason by the
  third and fourth questions in
  [presenters/README.md](../../README.md#the-same-question-type-several-presenters-playbooks).

How to draw the big-picture map, the beat of a crossroads, zooming in and out, honesty, and names and years are in her
script craft ([craft.md](../craft.md)); the picture is in the style ([style.md](../style.md)). In this playbook the
big-picture map has two layers: underneath, the table where it was designed (a seat chart, with each side's goal cards);
on top, the finished system (rows of seats, or a clause card when a part is a clause; the whole thing first `dim`, one
part `undim` at each crossroads, the options not chosen with `get_alternative`). For the walk-through and swapping a
part, the table shrinks to a corner and the system is enlarged.

## Skeleton

| Share | Section | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0–8% | The whole picture first | A strange fact today (Rhode Island has two senators, just like California) → the whole finished system drawn faintly, the main question in one sentence ("Why was it designed this way?") → "it was designed in ___, at one table" |
| 8–18% | That table | The seat chart: who was at the table, each with a goal card (large states, small states, slave states); which failure they had just come from (where the previous version of the system broke, a one-minute short version) |
| 18–65% | One part, one crossroads: three to five | One beat each: that part of the system lights up (still faint) → the options on the table as two or three arrows, one sentence each → guess first who will want which, and why (the faint whole picture already shows the answer, so do not guess which was chosen), a 3-second pause → why this one was chosen (using only what was known at the time) → that part is filled in solid, and the options not chosen are drawn as dashed versions beside it → "so": what this part is responsible for. For the most important one, say so plainly |
| 65–80% | Follow one real thing through | The table leaves; one real thing goes from the system's entrance to its exit, each station lighting up, saying what it did and which crossroads left it there |
| 80–92% | Swap one part | Pick a crossroads, swap the dashed version in, and walk the same thing through again: who gains, who loses, using the consequences people feared back then or numbers historians have worked out; or the time it really was changed later, and whom that affected |
| 92–100% | Close | The system replays, each part showing the year of its crossroads beside it; answer the opening's strange fact; ask the viewer about a part never reached: what is it responsible for, and whom would a change to it affect? |

Measured pace (the same explainer on America's first constitution, its passage on how the new constitution was
designed, about 9 minutes 45 seconds): the changeover 45 seconds (8%), lesson one 45 seconds (8%), lesson two 4 minutes
15 seconds (43%), lesson three 3 minutes 10 seconds (32%), the overall diagram 50 seconds (8%), with the costs a further
minute and a half; one more icon on screen every 5 to 6 seconds, one part (one card) about 20 to 40 seconds. Another, on
NATO, gives the design only 40 seconds. Clio: one part per beat, 1 to 1.5 minutes per beat (with the options and the
guess added); three to five parts are enough, the others passed over in one sentence during the walk-through.

## Short version (6 minutes or less)

- The whole picture in one diagram, two crossroads, one real thing walked through, one sentence on swapping a part.
- The whole picture first and swapping a part can never be cut.

## Quality bar

After watching, given a part, the viewer can say:

- what it is responsible for;
- which other option was on the table back then, who wanted it, and why it was not chosen;
- if it were swapped for that option (or after it really was changed later), who would gain and who would lose.

Remembering only a list of design terms, or only the course of the constitutional convention, does not count.

## Self-check (in addition to the craft self-check)

- [ ] In the first minute the finished system is drawn faintly on the big-picture map, and the main question asks "why
      was it designed this way", not "how did it come about".
- [ ] Three to five parts, each with its options, who wanted which, a guess first and the reasons of the time laid out;
      no section reads the design questions out as a list.
- [ ] Every option not chosen stays as a dashed version beside its part.
- [ ] What is followed through is one real thing, passing every filled-in part.
- [ ] Swapping a part uses the consequences people feared back then, numbers historians have worked out, or the time it
      really was changed later; not our own extrapolation.
- [ ] Every side at the design table has a goal card, the losing side too.
